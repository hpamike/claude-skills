# Regulatory Context

Smuggler and Commissioner tactics are not only ethically suspect — they are increasingly illegal or subject to enforcement. Use this context when the audit audience is conversion-focused and needs the business case for removing dark patterns, not just the ethical one.

The information below is a general overview, not legal advice. For actual enforcement decisions, link to the source and let the user's legal counsel interpret.

## European Union

### Digital Services Act (DSA, in force 2024)

Article 25 prohibits online platforms from designing interfaces that "deceive or manipulate" users or "materially distort or impair" their ability to make free and informed choices. The European Commission has published guidance naming specific patterns as non-compliant, including:

- Giving more visual prominence to one option over another in consent flows
- Repeatedly asking users to reconsider a choice they've already made
- Making a less-preferred-by-the-platform option harder to select
- Requiring more steps to decline than to accept

Scope: "very large online platforms" (VLOPs) and "very large online search engines" first, with broader reach through member-state implementations.

### Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC) with dark-pattern guidance (2021)

The European Commission's 2021 guidance on the UCPD applies the Directive's prohibition on misleading actions to specific dark patterns, including false urgency ("only today"), false scarcity ("last one"), and false social proof.

### Consumer Protection Cooperation (CPC) actions

The CPC network has taken coordinated action against travel platforms for misleading urgency and scarcity claims — notably the 2019–2020 dialogue that led Booking.com and others to revise "only X rooms left" and countdown messaging.

### Dutch ACM (Autoriteit Consument & Markt)

The ACM has published explicit guidance on "online misleiding" and has enforced against:

- False scarcity cues on e-commerce
- Misleading countdown timers
- Hidden subscription terms
- Fake reviews and review manipulation

Fines are possible; reputational exposure in Dutch press is often the more immediate cost.

## United States

### FTC unfair and deceptive practices (FTC Act §5)

The FTC treats material misrepresentations to consumers as deceptive. Enforcement against dark patterns has included:

- Fake reviews and endorsements (Endorsement Guides updated 2023; Trade Regulation Rule on fake reviews finalized 2024)
- Negative-option marketing (automatic renewals, sign-up/cancel asymmetry) under the "Click-to-Cancel" rule
- Drip pricing and hidden fees
- "Free" offers with undisclosed material conditions

### FTC workshop on dark patterns (2021)

The FTC's *Bringing Dark Patterns to Light* report enumerates specific practices and has informed subsequent rulemaking. Named practices are closer to automatic enforcement risk.

## United Kingdom

### CMA (Competition and Markets Authority)

Ongoing enforcement on online choice architecture. The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 gives the CMA direct enforcement powers on consumer law, with fines up to 10% of global turnover for breaches, including misleading omissions and false urgency.

### ASA (Advertising Standards Authority)

Adjudicates on misleading advertising claims, including unsubstantiated social proof and scarcity messaging.

## When to cite which regulator

Match the regulator to the audience:

- **Dutch or Belgian brand** — ACM rulings and EU CPC actions are most salient.
- **EU-wide platform or service** — DSA guidance and UCPD enforcement.
- **US brand** — FTC deception framework and the 2024 fake-review rule.
- **UK brand** — CMA powers under the 2024 Act.
- **Global consumer brand** — note that once a practice is flagged in any major jurisdiction, multi-jurisdictional enforcement often follows within 12–24 months.

## Strategic framing

The regulatory exposure is not uniform across the three classifications:

- **Detective** tactics carry essentially no regulatory risk.
- **Smuggler** tactics carry moderate risk, rising with prominence of the misleading framing and the ease with which a consumer could be misled.
- **Commissioner** tactics carry the highest risk. Fabricated reviews, fake countdowns, invented certifications, and sham scarcity have drawn fines, settlements, or orders in every major jurisdiction above.

In audit recommendations, explicitly call out Commissioner tactics as legal risks and suggest the substitution to the Detective form where possible. This reframes the conversation away from "is this ethical" (which stakeholders sometimes contest) toward "how much legal and reputational exposure does this carry" (which they cannot).
